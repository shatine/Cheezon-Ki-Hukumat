How did they tunnel through a mountain gushing water?

Atal Tunnel carries traffic through 9.02 km of the Himalayas. Yet the contractor recorded peak inflow of up to 8,000 litres per minute in the Seri Nala zone, with mud and broken rock coming in too. According to the client's records, excavating the upper part through a difficult stretch of less than 600 metres took about four years. How did engineers keep a working space open inside that ground?

Pumping out water did not make the loose roof and walls strong. Engineers reinforced the ground ahead with pipe roofs, excavated in short advances and supported newly exposed surfaces immediately. During upper excavation, a temporary invert connected the supports. Later, the remaining lower ground was excavated and the difficult zone received its deep final invert: a later stage, not the same temporary floor.

Water needed its own route. Grouting reduced inflow where appropriate, while major inflows were collected and drained. Blocking a large water path could increase pressure behind the structure. Monitoring ground movement helped engineers review the excavation and support. Breakthrough came in 2017; the tunnel opened on 3 October 2020. A site engineer's 2024 retrospective also described leakage-management difficulties after opening. That is a historical observation, not a statement about conditions today.
